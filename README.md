## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

Provenance Guard is a web service that takes a piece of text and tells a reader
how likely it is to have been written by a person or generated by AI. Every
decision is logged, and a writer who disagrees can appeal. A submission takes
seven steps from arriving to the label a reader sees:

1. **The route (`POST /submit`, `app.py`).** Receives the JSON
   `{"text": "...", "creator_id": "..."}`. It checks the input first: missing or
   empty text is rejected with a 400 error instead of crashing. (There is no
   length cap yet, and very short text still fails inside the model signal;
   both are known gaps.) It adds a unique `content_id`, which every later step
   and any appeal hangs off.
2. **Signal one: the model signal (`detector.model_signal`).** Runs the text
   through a local language model (gpt2) and measures how predictable it is
   (perplexity). It adds `model_score`, from 0 to 1, where higher means more
   likely AI. It reads the text for meaning-level predictability and knows
   nothing about its shape.
3. **Signal two: the style signal (`stylometry.style_signal`).** Measures the
   text as text, without reading it: how much sentence length varies, how much
   vocabulary repeats, and how punctuation is used. It adds `style_score`, from
   0 to 1, where higher means more likely AI. It knows nothing about what the
   text says.
4. **The combining rule (`scoring.combine_signals`).** Merges `model_score` and
   `style_score` into one `combined_score` from 0 to 1. This is where the two
   signals' disagreements get resolved, and where the decision about which kind
   of mistake to risk is made.
5. **The label mapping (`scoring.score_to_label`).** Compares the combined score
   with the thresholds in `config.py` and adds a `guess` (`human`, `unsure` or
   `ai`) and a plain-English label sentence written for someone who has never
   heard the word "threshold".
6. **The audit log (`audit.log_decision`).** Writes a permanent record: the
   `content_id`, `creator_id`, both signal scores, the combined score, the label
   and a status of `decided`. Nothing is judged without leaving a trace.
7. **The response.** Returns `content_id`, `guess`, `confidence`, `label`,
   `model_score` and `style_score` to the caller. Both signal scores are included
   so that failures can later be traced to a stage.

**The appeal path.** If a writer disagrees, they send `POST /appeal` with the
`content_id` and their reasoning. The service checks the `content_id` exists
(`audit.entries_for`), records the appeal with `audit.log_appeal`, and moves the
item's status from `decided` to `under_review`. It then confirms to the writer
that the appeal was received.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to a small language model
(gpt2, run locally). This is perplexity: low means the model could have
guessed the next words, high means it was surprised. `detector.model_signal`
turns that into a 0–1 score where higher means more likely AI.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** A language model
writes by repeatedly choosing likely next words, so text it generates tends to
be text another model finds easy to predict. Human writing wanders more, with
odd word choices, abrupt turns and specific details instead of typical ones.

**What it can't see:** Authorship. Predictability is not the same as being
machine-written. The signal rewards common words in short, ordinary sentences,
and plenty of real people write that way: someone writing in a second
language and sticking to vocabulary they're sure of, a younger writer, anyone
writing plainly on purpose. They score as machine-like. The reverse also
holds: ornate prose full of rare words reads as human, so AI text padded with
unusual vocabulary slips through.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text, without reading it: how much
sentence length varies (`sentence_length_spread`), how much of the vocabulary
repeats (`type_token_ratio`), and how much and how varied the punctuation is
(`punctuation_density`). `stylometry.style_signal` combines these into a 0–1
score where higher means more likely AI.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose tends
toward even, mid-length sentences and a narrower band of vocabulary, because
that is what "most likely next word" produces on average. Human writing is
lumpier, with a three-word sentence next to a forty-word one.

**What it can't see:** Meaning. It has no idea what the text says, so anything
that changes the shape without changing the substance moves it: adding typos
(which raise vocabulary variety), breaking sentences up or joining them,
pasting in a quotation. All of these are cheap to do on purpose. It also
misjudges writers whose natural style is even and repetitive, such as short
plain sentences or a formal template.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** A weighted average of the two signals, with the model signal
counting more because the style signal is easier to game. On top of that, the
"AI" label needs both signals to lean AI: if either signal is below 0.5, the
combined score is held just under the AI threshold, so it can say "unsure" but
never "AI". A wrong accusation costs a real writer more than a missed detection
costs the site, so one signal alone can never accuse anyone.

**The numbers:** `combined = 0.6 * model_score + 0.4 * style_score`. If
`min(model_score, style_score) < 0.5`, `combined` is capped at 0.64 (the AI
threshold of 0.65 minus 0.01). The weights and the 0.5 bar live in `config.py`
(`WEIGHT_MODEL_SIGNAL`, `WEIGHT_STYLE_SIGNAL`, `AI_REQUIRES_BOTH_AT`). The style
signal itself is `0.5 * spread + 0.2 * vocabulary + 0.3 * punctuation`, each
turned into an AI-likeness from 0 to 1 (see `stylometry.py::style_signal`).

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

Measured on my four calibration inputs plus a plain human paragraph (texts I
wrote, so treat them as a rough check):

| Input | model | style | combined | guess |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| clearly AI | 0.665 | 0.271 | 0.508 | unsure |
| clearly human | 0.328 | 0.070 | 0.225 | human |
| plain human | 0.843 | 0.454 | 0.640 | unsure |
| formal human | 0.463 | 0.000 | 0.278 | human |
| lightly-edited AI | 0.534 | 0.297 | 0.439 | unsure |

The clearest split is the **clearly-AI paragraph**: the model signal says 0.665
(AI) but the style signal says 0.271 (human-ish), because its long sentences
contain plenty of commas, which my punctuation measure reads as human. The rule
averages them to 0.508 and, since the style signal is below 0.5, would have
capped the score under the AI threshold anyway: the result is "unsure". I
accept missing an AI text here rather than accusing on one signal.

**A change I made after seeing a false positive.** My first version of the
style signal treated a lack of punctuation as evidence of AI. On a plain human
paragraph (short, even sentences, only full stops) both signals agreed it was
AI (0.843 and 0.604, combined 0.748, labelled AI), which the combining rule
could not stop because both signals were above 0.5. I changed the punctuation
measure so that having few marks is neutral (0.5) rather than AI-like: plain
writers and second-language writers use few marks too, so absence is not
evidence. The same paragraph now scores style 0.454 and combined 0.640 and is
labelled "unsure". I made this change because the measure was unsound, not to
move one number, but the cost is real: none of my five test texts now reaches
the "AI" label, so I still need a genuinely AI-written text to show that label
is reachable (criterion 3). The plain writer is protected only by the guard
in the combining rule and a style score just under 0.5, which is thin.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.35 | We think this was probably written by a person. |
| unsure | 0.35 – 0.65 | We can't tell whether this was written by a person or with the help of AI. Our check isn't always right, especially on plain, simple writing, so please don't take this as a verdict. If you wrote it yourself, you can appeal and a person will look at it. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.65 – 1.00 | This may have been written with the help of AI. Our check can be wrong. If you wrote it yourself, you can appeal and a person will review it. |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**

It is the label a real writer is most likely to meet, and they may have written
every word themselves, so it never says or implies they cheated. It says "we
can't tell" rather than "this might be AI", names the limit of the check
("isn't always right, especially on plain, simple writing", which is true and
is the writer I traced in Milestone 1), and tells them not to take it as a
verdict. It ends with something they can do: appeal, and a person will look.
The other two labels carry the same appeal line, because either one could be
wrong. None of them shows a number, because "0.64" means nothing to a writer.

**Reachability.** I submitted text that produced each label through
`POST /submit`: a clearly AI-style paragraph scored 0.728 (AI), a messy
personal paragraph scored 0.225 (human), and a plain paragraph about a hike
scored 0.640 (unsure). All three are reachable. One caution: the AI label was
reached only by a paragraph of short, flat sentences, which is also what plain
human writing looks like. Other AI-style paragraphs I tried scored 0.635 to
0.640 and were held at "unsure" by the rule that both signals must agree.

## How I Used AI

**Moment 1**

- *What I asked for:* After I had written the `/submit` route myself, with the
  signal wired in, I asked Claude whether I needed to change anything else.
- *What came back:* A review of my own code. It pointed out that `detector`
  wasn't imported, that `model_signal` ran *before* my empty-text check (so an
  empty submission would crash with a 500 before my 400 could fire), and that
  the model ran twice, once for the log and once for the response, which is
  slow and can give two different numbers. A later check found I had also
  deleted the `model_score` line while the log call still used it, which
  raised a `NameError` on every valid request.
- *What I changed:* I restored `model_score`, moved the model call below the
  validation check, and used the one variable in both the log and the
  response. After that a valid request returned a real score and landed in
  `/log`, and an empty `text` got a clean 400.
