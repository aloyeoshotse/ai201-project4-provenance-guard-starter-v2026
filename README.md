## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

Provenance Guard is a web service that takes a piece of text and tells a reader
how likely it is to have been written by a person or generated by AI. Every
decision is logged, and a writer who disagrees can appeal. A submission takes
seven steps from arriving to the label a reader sees:

1. **The route (`POST /submit`, `app.py`).** Receives the JSON
   `{"text": "...", "creator_id": "..."}`. It checks the input first: missing,
   empty or enormous text is rejected with an error instead of crashing. It adds
   a unique `content_id`, which every later step and any appeal hangs off.
2. **Signal one: the model signal (`detector.model_signal`).** Runs the text
   through a local language model (gpt2) and measures how predictable it is
   (perplexity). It adds `model_score`, from 0 to 1, where higher means more
   likely AI. It reads the text for meaning-level predictability and knows
   nothing about its shape.
3. **Signal two: the style signal (`stylometry.style_signal`).** Measures the
   text as text, without reading it: how much sentence length varies, how much
   vocabulary repeats, and how punctuation is used. It adds `style_score`, from
   0 to 1, where higher means more likely AI. It knows nothing about what the
   text says.
4. **The combining rule (`scoring.combine_signals`).** Merges `model_score` and
   `style_score` into one `combined_score` from 0 to 1. This is where the two
   signals' disagreements get resolved, and where the decision about which kind
   of mistake to risk is made.
5. **The label mapping (`scoring.score_to_label`).** Compares the combined score
   with the thresholds in `config.py` and adds a `guess` (`human`, `unsure` or
   `ai`) and a plain-English label sentence written for someone who has never
   heard the word "threshold".
6. **The audit log (`audit.log_decision`).** Writes a permanent record: the
   `content_id`, `creator_id`, both signal scores, the combined score, the label
   and a status of `decided`. Nothing is judged without leaving a trace.
7. **The response.** Returns `content_id`, `guess`, `confidence`, `label`,
   `model_score` and `style_score` to the caller. Both signal scores are included
   so that failures can later be traced to a stage.

**The appeal path.** If a writer disagrees, they send `POST /appeal` with the
`content_id` and their reasoning. The service checks the `content_id` exists
(`audit.entries_for`), records the appeal with `audit.log_appeal`, and moves the
item's status from `decided` to `under_review`. It then confirms to the writer
that the appeal was received.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to a small language model
(gpt2, run locally). This is perplexity: low means the model could have
guessed the next words, high means it was surprised. `detector.model_signal`
turns that into a 0–1 score where higher means more likely AI.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** A language model
writes by repeatedly choosing likely next words, so text it generates tends to
be text another model finds easy to predict. Human writing wanders more, with
odd word choices, abrupt turns and specific details instead of typical ones.

**What it can't see:** Authorship. Predictability is not the same as being
machine-written. The signal rewards common words in short, ordinary sentences,
and plenty of real people write that way: someone writing in a second
language and sticking to vocabulary they're sure of, a younger writer, anyone
writing plainly on purpose. They score as machine-like. The reverse also
holds: ornate prose full of rare words reads as human, so AI text padded with
unusual vocabulary slips through.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text, without reading it: how much
sentence length varies (`sentence_length_spread`), how much of the vocabulary
repeats (`type_token_ratio`), and how much and how varied the punctuation is
(`punctuation_density`). `stylometry.style_signal` combines these into a 0–1
score where higher means more likely AI.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated prose tends
toward even, mid-length sentences and a narrower band of vocabulary, because
that is what "most likely next word" produces on average. Human writing is
lumpier, with a three-word sentence next to a forty-word one.

**What it can't see:** Meaning. It has no idea what the text says, so anything
that changes the shape without changing the substance moves it: adding typos
(which raise vocabulary variety), breaking sentences up or joining them,
pasting in a quotation. All of these are cheap to do on purpose. It also
misjudges writers whose natural style is even and repetitive, such as short
plain sentences or a formal template.
